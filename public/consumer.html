Consumer 2036

What people could pay for in 2036

In 2036, people are likely to pay for food that feels made for them, delivers an exceptional experience, and helps them participate in occasions they value. Health can strengthen that proposition, but the immediate reward will usually remain taste, convenience, confidence or social connection.

The change from today could be how precisely and conveniently those benefits are delivered. These are plausible scenarios, not predictions of guaranteed demand.

Imagine a personalised café breakfast

The most interesting 2036 product could combine several of these benefits.

  • It is ready when you arrive.
  • The drink has your preferred flavour and sweetness.
  • The food provides a clearly specified nutritional contribution.
  • It looks and tastes like something you would choose for pleasure.
  • You can enjoy it with colleagues without explaining a complicated diet.
  • The total price remains acceptable compared with your usual breakfast.

The customer pays for a breakfast that fits their life. The ingredient supplier enables that experience through taste, texture, nutrition and consistency. A wearable could contribute information where useful, but owning one need not be a condition of purchase.

What might become “fancy”, and what could become ordinary?

My scenario is that personalisation splits into two markets:

Everyday personalisationPremium personalisation
Choosing among a few well-designed recipesA highly customised recipe or preparation
Preferences remembered automaticallySpecial consultations or testing where justified
Familiar food at a competitive priceExclusive flavours, craftsmanship and theatrical service
Convenience is the main rewardExperience, recognition and exclusivity justify the premium

An AI recommendation may become commonplace. The scarce value could be the ability to deliver the recommended food deliciously, consistently and conveniently.

Where I would be sceptical

I would be more sceptical of products sold mainly as:

  • “AI-created” without a noticeable improvement.
  • “Personalised” when the recommendation is generic.
  • “Longevity food” with an expensive but uncertain promise.
  • A subscription requiring constant tracking and effort.
  • An attractive health score that does not establish a meaningful benefit.

The strongest 2036 proposition for the ingredients industry

“We make desirable experiences possible while improving the nutritional formulation.”

Consumers may pay the brand or restaurant for the experience; manufacturers then pay ingredient suppliers for the capabilities that deliver it.
