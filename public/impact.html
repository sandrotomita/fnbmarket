Flavours capture more reformulation value

2036 hypothesis: more business comes from preserving a sensory promise under changing constraints.

Customer problemWhat the flavour supplier sellsWhat determines value
Less sugar or sodiumA complete taste system with masking, aroma and mouthfeel supportConsumer liking at the target nutrient level
Variable protein sourceA validated flavour system for more than one base recipePerformance after real processing and storage
Scarce botanical supplyQualified source substitutions and alternative aroma routesTaste consistency, legal label and supply assurance
Stricter evidence demandsDocumentation tied to each formula version and intended useFaster customer qualification and fewer relaunches

Commercial model inference: suppliers may earn development fees, licence proprietary systems or bundle services into ingredient pricing. Open ingredient catalogues remain relevant, but application data and trusted dossiers can increase switching costs. Risks include customer demands for data portability, commoditisation of basic AI tools and margin pressure from procurement. Premium origin-based extracts can coexist with biomanufactured molecules.

Three products that make 2036 concrete

2036 product briefIngredient systemBusiness effect
Citrus soft drink. Target: 4 g sugar/100 mlApproved sweetener blend, acidity balance, citrus aroma and body adjustmentMulti-market recipes. Validate preservation, liking and shelf life.
Creamy protein dessert. Target: 15 g protein/servingPermitted plant or fermentation protein, texture system and bitterness controlRegional protein choices. Validate allergens, heat stability and repeat purchase.
Smoky savoury snack. Target: 20% less sodium than its referenceCompliant flavour system, seasoning distribution and saltiness enhancementRequalify seasoning and label. Confirm taste and process consistency.

Fictional development briefs. Targets illustrate the scenario and are not validated recipes or health claims, nor recommendations for consumer diets. Soft-drink tax exposure depends on jurisdiction, recipe and future law, not the 4 g target alone. Protein permission, naming and allergen status are ingredient-specific. The sodium reference must be defined before making a comparative claim.

The impact across food and beverages

Strategic inferences for the central 2036 scenario.

Industry segmentLikely change in demandOperating consequence
BeveragesSweetness systems, aroma protection and stable coloursMore sensory work. Market-specific recipes.
Dairy and alternativesProteins, cultures, enzymes and texture systemsNew process controls and allergen segregation.
Snacks, sauces and mealsSodium-reduction systems and replacement flavoursSeasoning requalification and shelf-life checks.
Bakery and confectioneryFunctional fibres, fat systems and resilient cocoa sourcingRedesign texture and moisture control.
Ingredient manufacturersApplication expertise, evidence services and qualified substitutesHigher data and compliance costs. More value in integrated systems.

Directional market analysis, not a measured demand forecast. Likely relative winners include suppliers that combine sensory expertise with regulatory evidence, efficient bioprocess developers with contracted demand and functional-fraction producers close to feedstock. Pressure increases on undifferentiated intermediaries and products whose claims or composition cannot withstand scrutiny. Consumer affordability can still overwhelm technically superior propositions.

Illustrative annual model of 100 million litres. Every price and cost is an assumption, not a forecast. Assumes unchanged sales and full tax benefit; excludes one-off costs. The assumed £0.20/L is hypothetical: it is not the current UK rate or a prediction of the 2036 rate. The break-even avoided levy at unchanged sales is £0.016/L before one-off costs (£1.6m/100m L). This case shows why total cost-in-use and regulation can matter more than ingredient price per kg.
